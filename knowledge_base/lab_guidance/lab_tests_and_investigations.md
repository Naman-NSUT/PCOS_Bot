# Lab Tests and Investigations — PCOS Health Assistant Knowledge Base

**Domain:** Medical Investigations & Lab Awareness  
**Purpose:** Help users understand what tests exist, why each is done, 
             what it measures, and when to ask their doctor for it.  
**Scope:** Educational awareness only. Never interpret specific user results.  
           Never suggest a diagnosis from results. Always recommend doctor review.

---

## SECTION 1 — WHY BLOOD TESTS MATTER IN PCOS

There is no single blood test that confirms PCOS on its own.
Diagnosis is always based on a combination of symptoms, medical history,
ultrasound findings, and lab results together.

Blood tests serve three purposes in PCOS:
1. Rule out other conditions that look like PCOS
2. Assess which hormones and metabolic markers are out of balance
3. Monitor health over time and catch complications early

Important timing note: Most hormone tests are most accurate when done
on days 2 to 5 of the menstrual cycle, fasting, and in the morning.
For irregular cycles, doctors usually advise a consistent time each month.
Always follow whatever instructions your doctor or lab gives you.

## SECTION 2 — HORMONE TESTS

### Testosterone (Total and Free)

What it is: The primary male hormone that women also produce in smaller amounts.
In PCOS, the ovaries often produce too much testosterone.

Why it matters: Elevated testosterone is one of the main diagnostic markers
for PCOS. It drives symptoms like acne, hirsutism (unwanted hair), 
hair thinning on the scalp, and irregular ovulation.

What to know: Total testosterone measures all testosterone in the blood.
Free testosterone measures only the portion that is active and available 
to affect body tissues. Free testosterone is often more telling.
Some women have normal total testosterone but elevated free testosterone —
this can still cause symptoms.

When to ask for it: If you have acne, unwanted facial or body hair, 
hair thinning, or irregular periods.

---

### SHBG — Sex Hormone Binding Globulin

What it is: A protein made by the liver that binds to testosterone and 
carries it through the blood. When SHBG is low, more testosterone is 
free and active in the body.

Why it matters: Women with PCOS and insulin resistance often have 
low SHBG. This means even if total testosterone looks normal on paper,
the amount actually acting on your tissues can be much higher.
Low SHBG is also a marker of insulin resistance.

What to know: Insulin resistance reduces SHBG production. 
So improving insulin sensitivity often raises SHBG naturally.

When to ask for it: Always alongside testosterone testing. 
The two together give a much clearer picture than either alone.

---

### LH — Luteinizing Hormone

What it is: A hormone made by the pituitary gland in the brain that 
triggers ovulation. In a typical cycle, LH surges mid-cycle to release an egg.

Why it matters: In PCOS, LH levels are often elevated, and LH pulses 
are more frequent than normal. This causes the ovaries to produce more 
androgens (male hormones) and disrupts normal follicle development.

What to know: LH is often tested alongside FSH. 
A higher-than-normal LH to FSH ratio is common in PCOS.
However this ratio is no longer used as a standalone diagnostic criterion
because it is not reliable enough on its own.

When to ask for it: As part of a hormonal workup for irregular cycles 
or fertility concerns. Best tested on days 2 to 5 of the cycle.

---

### FSH — Follicle Stimulating Hormone

What it is: Another pituitary hormone that stimulates the growth of 
follicles in the ovaries. Each follicle contains a developing egg.

Why it matters: In PCOS, FSH levels are usually normal or slightly low.
FSH is also tested to rule out primary ovarian insufficiency, 
which causes its own set of symptoms that can overlap with PCOS.

What to know: Elevated FSH can indicate declining ovarian reserve.
Normal or low FSH with elevated LH points more toward PCOS.

When to ask for it: When investigating irregular cycles or 
fertility concerns. Always tested alongside LH.

---

### AMH — Anti-Müllerian Hormone

What it is: A hormone produced by small follicles in the ovaries.
It reflects how many follicles a woman has — her ovarian reserve.

Why it matters: Women with PCOS typically have significantly elevated AMH
because they have a higher-than-normal number of small follicles.
AMH is now included in the 2023 international PCOS guidelines as 
an alternative to ultrasound for assessing polycystic ovary morphology.

What to know: Elevated AMH on its own does not diagnose PCOS.
It must be considered alongside other symptoms and test results.
AMH also gives useful information about fertility potential —
higher AMH generally means more eggs remaining.

When to ask for it: When investigating PCOS, fertility, or ovarian reserve.
Unlike most hormone tests, AMH can be tested on any day of the cycle.

---

### Prolactin

What it is: A hormone that stimulates milk production after pregnancy.
In non-pregnant women it should be at low baseline levels.

Why it matters: Elevated prolactin (hyperprolactinemia) can cause 
irregular periods and symptoms that look exactly like PCOS.
This is one of the conditions that must be ruled out before 
a PCOS diagnosis is made.

What to know: A small number of women with PCOS do have mildly elevated
prolactin. Significantly elevated prolactin, however, usually points to 
a different cause — sometimes a small benign pituitary growth called a 
prolactinoma, which is treatable.

When to ask for it: Always as part of the initial PCOS investigation 
workup to rule out hyperprolactinemia.

---

### DHEA-S — Dehydroepiandrosterone Sulfate

What it is: An androgen produced primarily by the adrenal glands 
(the small glands that sit on top of your kidneys), not the ovaries.

Why it matters: Mildly elevated DHEA-S is common in PCOS.
Very high DHEA-S can indicate the adrenal glands rather than the 
ovaries are the primary source of excess androgens, or in rare cases
it can point to an adrenal tumor.

What to know: DHEA-S levels above 800 mcg/dL are considered very high
and warrant further investigation. Mild elevations are common in PCOS.

When to ask for it: As part of the androgen panel, especially 
if hirsutism is prominent or testosterone is very high.

---

### Androstenedione

What it is: A precursor hormone that converts to both testosterone 
and estrogen in the body. Produced by both the ovaries and adrenal glands.

Why it matters: Elevated androstenedione is found in many women with PCOS.
It contributes to the overall androgen excess picture.

When to ask for it: As part of a full androgen panel. 
Often tested alongside testosterone and DHEA-S.

---

### Estradiol (E2)

What it is: The main form of estrogen produced by the ovaries.

Why it matters: Helps assess overall ovarian function and 
is useful context alongside LH and FSH testing.
Women with PCOS often have adequate estrogen levels, 
unlike women with ovarian insufficiency who have low estrogen.

When to ask for it: Alongside LH and FSH on day 2 to 5 of the cycle.

---

### Progesterone

What it is: A hormone produced after ovulation by the corpus luteum 
(the follicle that released the egg).

Why it matters: If progesterone is measured mid-luteal phase 
(about 7 days before the expected period) and is low or absent,
it confirms that ovulation did not occur that cycle.
This is an objective way to assess whether cycles are truly anovulatory.

When to ask for it: If you have regular-looking periods but suspect 
you may not be ovulating. Your doctor will time this test carefully.

---

### 17-Hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP)

What it is: A hormone involved in adrenal steroid production.

Why it matters: Elevated 17-OHP can indicate a condition called 
non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia (NCAH) — 
a genetic condition that closely mimics PCOS in symptoms
and must be ruled out as part of the diagnostic process.

What to know: This test is done in the early follicular phase 
(days 2 to 5 of the cycle) for the most accurate result.

When to ask for it: Always as part of the initial PCOS workup
to rule out NCAH. Especially important if hirsutism is severe.

## SECTION 3 — THYROID TESTS

### TSH — Thyroid Stimulating Hormone

What it is: A hormone made by the pituitary gland that controls
how active the thyroid gland is.

Why it matters: Thyroid disorders — both underactive (hypothyroidism) 
and overactive (hyperthyroidism) — can cause symptoms almost identical 
to PCOS: irregular periods, weight changes, fatigue, hair changes, 
and mood disturbances.
TSH must be tested in every PCOS workup to rule out thyroid dysfunction.

What to know: If TSH is abnormal, the thyroid, not PCOS, 
may be the primary cause of symptoms, or both conditions 
may exist at the same time — which is not uncommon.

When to ask for it: Always. It is a standard part of any 
hormonal or PCOS investigation.

---

### Free T3 and Free T4

What it is: The active thyroid hormones circulating in the blood.

Why it matters: TSH alone sometimes misses borderline thyroid issues.
Free T3 and T4 give a more complete picture of how the thyroid is functioning.
Thyroid antibodies (anti-TPO) can also be tested to detect 
autoimmune thyroid conditions like Hashimoto's disease.

When to ask for it: If TSH is borderline or symptoms of thyroid 
dysfunction are prominent alongside PCOS-like symptoms.

---

## SECTION 4 — METABOLIC AND INSULIN TESTS

### Fasting Blood Glucose

What it is: Blood sugar level measured after not eating for 8 to 12 hours.

Why it matters: Women with PCOS have a significantly higher risk of 
developing prediabetes and type 2 diabetes due to insulin resistance.
Fasting glucose is a basic screen for blood sugar dysregulation.

What to know: Normal fasting glucose is below 100 mg/dL.
100 to 125 mg/dL indicates prediabetes.
126 mg/dL or above on two tests indicates diabetes.
However, fasting glucose alone can miss insulin resistance 
in its earlier stages — fasting insulin is more sensitive.

When to ask for it: Annually as part of metabolic monitoring in PCOS,
or sooner if you have risk factors like family history of diabetes, 
weight gain, or sugar cravings.

---

### Fasting Insulin

What it is: The level of insulin in the blood after fasting.

Why it matters: This is one of the most important tests for PCOS 
that is frequently overlooked. Around 70% of women with PCOS 
have insulin resistance, but fasting glucose alone often 
appears normal even when insulin resistance is present.

Elevated fasting insulin means the body is producing excess insulin 
to compensate for reduced insulin sensitivity — a precursor to 
prediabetes and worsened hormonal symptoms.

What to know: Fasting insulin above 8 to 10 μIU/mL is considered 
a warning sign. Above 12 to 15 μIU/mL is significantly elevated.
Reference ranges vary between labs — always ask your doctor 
to interpret in context.

When to ask for it: Alongside fasting glucose. 
Always fast for at least 10 to 12 hours before this test.

---

### HOMA-IR — Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance

What it is: A calculated score using fasting glucose and fasting insulin
to estimate the degree of insulin resistance.

Formula: HOMA-IR = (fasting insulin × fasting glucose) / 405
(when glucose is in mg/dL and insulin is in μIU/mL)

Why it matters: Gives a single number to quantify insulin resistance.
A HOMA-IR below 1.0 indicates good insulin sensitivity.
1.0 to 1.9 is borderline.
Above 1.9 suggests insulin resistance.
Above 2.9 suggests significant insulin resistance.

What to know: Your doctor may not routinely calculate this —
you can ask for it to be calculated from your fasting glucose 
and insulin results.

When to ask for it: Whenever fasting glucose and insulin are tested.

---

### HbA1c — Glycated Hemoglobin

What it is: A measure of average blood sugar over the past 2 to 3 months.
It reflects long-term blood sugar control rather than a single moment.

Why it matters: Useful for monitoring diabetes risk over time.
Does not require fasting. Can be done at any time of day.

What to know: Below 5.7% is normal.
5.7% to 6.4% indicates prediabetes.
6.5% or above indicates diabetes.
HbA1c can miss early insulin resistance — fasting insulin 
is still needed alongside it.

When to ask for it: Annually in PCOS, or every 3 to 6 months
if blood sugar markers have been elevated.

---

### OGTT — Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (2-hour)

What it is: A test where you drink a 75-gram glucose solution 
after fasting, and blood is drawn at intervals (fasting, 1 hour, 2 hours)
to see how your body clears glucose.

Why it matters: The most sensitive test for detecting glucose 
dysregulation in PCOS. Catches impaired glucose tolerance 
that fasting glucose and HbA1c completely miss.

Some clinics also draw insulin levels at each interval 
to detect hyperinsulinemia — where insulin spikes very high 
even when glucose is in normal range. This can occur years 
before glucose levels become abnormal.

What to know: 2-hour glucose below 140 mg/dL is normal.
140 to 199 mg/dL indicates impaired glucose tolerance (prediabetes).
200 mg/dL or above indicates diabetes.

When to ask for it: If you have risk factors for diabetes 
(family history, weight gain, elevated fasting glucose or insulin), 
or if your doctor wants a complete picture of glucose metabolism.

## SECTION 5 — LIPID PANEL

### What it includes:
- Total cholesterol
- LDL cholesterol (low density — often called the "bad" cholesterol)
- HDL cholesterol (high density — often called the "good" cholesterol)
- Triglycerides

### Why it matters in PCOS:

Women with PCOS commonly have an unfavorable lipid pattern including:
- Elevated triglycerides
- Low HDL cholesterol
- Higher LDL cholesterol
- A triglyceride to HDL ratio above 2 — which is a marker of 
  insulin resistance and cardiovascular risk

This lipid pattern increases long-term cardiovascular risk 
and can develop well before any heart disease symptoms appear.

### What to know:

The TG:HDL ratio (triglycerides divided by HDL) is a particularly 
useful indicator of insulin resistance in PCOS.
A ratio close to 1.0 reflects good metabolic health.
A ratio above 2.0 suggests insulin resistance is likely.
A ratio above 3.0 is significantly elevated.

### When to ask for it:

Annually as part of cardiovascular risk monitoring in PCOS.
Always fasting (8 to 12 hours) for accurate triglyceride results.

## SECTION 6 — PREGNANCY TEST

### hCG — Human Chorionic Gonadotropin

What it is: The hormone produced in early pregnancy.

Why it matters: Missed or irregular periods are common in PCOS
but can also be caused by pregnancy. A pregnancy test must always 
be done first before investigating PCOS or starting any treatment,
to ensure the cause of missed periods is not pregnancy.

When to ask for it: Whenever there are missed or very irregular periods.

---

## SECTION 7 — ADDITIONAL MONITORING TESTS

### Vitamin D

What it is: A fat-soluble vitamin that acts like a hormone in the body.

Why it matters: Vitamin D deficiency is extremely common in PCOS
and is associated with worsened insulin resistance, mood disturbances,
and inflammatory markers. Many women with PCOS are deficient.

When to ask for it: As part of a full nutritional assessment.
Especially if you have fatigue, low mood, or poor immune function.

---

### CRP — C-Reactive Protein (high sensitivity)

What it is: A marker of systemic inflammation produced by the liver.

Why it matters: PCOS is associated with chronic low-grade inflammation
that is now recognized as part of its underlying mechanism.
Elevated hs-CRP indicates this inflammatory state and is 
also a cardiovascular risk marker.

When to ask for it: As part of cardiovascular risk assessment or 
to understand the inflammatory component of PCOS.

---

### Liver Enzymes — AST and ALT

What it is: Markers of liver health.

Why it matters: Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease 
(MASLD, previously called NAFLD) is more common in women with PCOS 
due to insulin resistance. Liver enzymes help monitor liver health.

When to ask for it: If metabolic risk factors are present — 
particularly insulin resistance, obesity, or elevated lipids.

---

### Complete Blood Count (CBC)

What it is: Measures red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, 
and hemoglobin.

Why it matters: Heavy or prolonged periods in PCOS can cause 
iron deficiency anemia. A CBC checks for this.

When to ask for it: If periods are heavy, prolonged, or 
if fatigue is a prominent symptom.

---

## SECTION 8 — IMAGING

### Pelvic Ultrasound

What it is: An imaging test using sound waves to visualize 
the ovaries and uterus. Can be done abdominally (over the belly) 
or transvaginally (internal, for a more detailed view).

Why it matters: Polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) is one of 
the three Rotterdam diagnostic criteria for PCOS.
On ultrasound this appears as 20 or more small follicles per ovary
and/or an ovarian volume greater than 10 mL.

What to know: The "cysts" in PCOS are not true cysts — 
they are small immature follicles that did not fully develop.
Not everyone with PCOS has visible polycystic ovaries on ultrasound,
and polycystic-looking ovaries can appear in women without PCOS.
Ultrasound findings must always be interpreted with clinical history.

Per the 2023 guidelines, AMH blood test can now be used 
as an alternative to ultrasound in adults when transvaginal 
ultrasound is not accessible or acceptable.

## SECTION 9 — HOW TO TALK TO YOUR DOCTOR ABOUT TESTING

If you are going to see a doctor about possible PCOS or 
hormonal concerns, here is what you can ask for:

**For initial investigation:**
- Testosterone (total and free, by LC-MS assay if possible)
- SHBG
- LH and FSH (days 2 to 5 of cycle)
- AMH
- Prolactin
- TSH
- 17-Hydroxyprogesterone
- Pregnancy test (hCG)

**For metabolic assessment:**
- Fasting glucose and fasting insulin
- HbA1c
- Full lipid panel
- Liver enzymes (AST, ALT)

**Optional but valuable:**
- DHEA-S
- Androstenedione
- Vitamin D
- hs-CRP
- OGTT with insulin levels (if insulin resistance is suspected)

**Imaging:**
- Pelvic ultrasound (transvaginal preferred for accuracy)

## SECTION 10 — IMPORTANT REMINDERS FOR USERS

- No single test diagnoses PCOS. It is always a combination of 
  clinical history, symptoms, and results together.

- Results within the "normal range" do not automatically mean 
  there is no issue — patterns and trends matter as much as 
  individual numbers.

- Timing matters. Most hormone tests should be done on days 2 to 5 
  of the menstrual cycle, fasting, in the morning.

- Hormonal contraceptives affect test results significantly. 
  If you are on the pill, results for testosterone, SHBG, LH, FSH,
  and AMH may not be interpretable. Do not stop contraception 
  without talking to your doctor first.

- Always bring your actual lab report to your doctor appointment, 
  not just a memory of the results. Numbers need to be interpreted 
  alongside reference ranges and your full clinical picture.

- Lab results should never be interpreted alone from the internet.
  This document is for educational awareness — helping you understand
  what tests are available and why they matter, so you can have 
  an informed conversation with your healthcare provider.
